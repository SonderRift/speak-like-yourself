Pronunciation

Brazilian Portuguese Pronunciation Guide (with Audio)

A complete Brazilian Portuguese pronunciation guide with audio, nasal vowels, the Brazilian R, the de/ti softening, and the rhythm that makes you sound native.

Rafael · Brazilian Portuguese teacherAugust 11, 2026 · 3 min read

Good news first: Brazilian Portuguese pronunciation is more consistent than English, once you learn the rules, you can read almost any word out loud correctly. The catch is that a few sounds don’t exist in English, and they’re exactly the ones that give beginners away. This guide walks through every one of them, with audio, so you can hear the difference and copy it. Master these and your accent jumps a level overnight.

The vowels: open, closed, and clear

Portuguese vowels are cleaner than English ones, no lazy “uh” sounds. But e and o each come in an open and a closed version. Avó (grandmother) has an open O; avô (grandfather) has a closed O, same spelling family, different mouth. The accent marks tell you: á, é, ó are open; â, ê, ô are closed. Listen and copy; your ear will learn this faster than your brain will.

Nasal vowels, the sound of Brazil

This is the Brazilian sound. When a vowel is followed by m, n, or a tilde (ã, õ), you send it up through your nose instead of fully closing your mouth. Não isn’t “now”, the air escapes through your nose. Pão (bread), mãe (mother), bem (well), sim (yes) all do it. Pinch your nose and say não, if the sound changes and your nose vibrates, you’re doing it right. Full drill in how to pronounce the nasal sounds.

The Brazilian R: when it sounds like an H

This one shocks beginners. At the start of a word, or when doubled, the Brazilian R is pronounced like a strong English H. Rio sounds like “HEE-oo,” carro like “KAH-hoo,” rato like “HAH-too.” But a single R between vowels is a soft tap, close to the R in the American “water”: caro (expensive) is “KAH-roo.” Same letter, two jobs, full guide in the Brazilian R sound.

Why ‘de’ sounds like ‘jee’ and ‘ti’ like ‘chee’

In most of Brazil, d and t get palatalized before an “ee” sound. So di and de (at the end of words) become “jee,” and ti and te become “chee.” Cidade → see-DAH-jee. Tia → CHEE-ah. Noite → NOY-chee. This single habit is the biggest accent upgrade you can make. Deep dive: why ‘de’ sounds like ‘jee’.

S, Z, LH and NH

A few more quick wins:

  • -s at the end of a syllable often sounds like “sh” in Rio (mais → “maish”), but like “s” in São Paulo. Both are correct, just different accents.
  • lh is the “lli” in “million”: filho (son) → “FEE-lyoo,” trabalho (work) → “trah-BAH-lyoo.”
  • nh is the “ny” in “canyon”: banho (bath) → “BAH-nyoo,” sonho (dream) → “SO-nyoo.”
  • ç (c-cedilha) is always a soft “s”: coração → “ko-rah-SOWN.”

Rhythm and stress: the music underneath

Portuguese is a stress-timed, sing-song language. Most words are stressed on the second-to-last syllable unless an accent mark says otherwise (obrigado → oh-bree-GAH-doo). Don’t clip your vowels the way English does, let them ring. If you get the melody right, listeners forgive a lot of small errors, because you sound Brazilian even before every sound is perfect. The numbers 1-100 guide is a great place to drill rhythm out loud.

The takeaway

Pronunciation is the fastest way to sound fluent, even while your grammar catches up. Focus on three things first, nasal vowels, the H-like R, and the de/ti = jee/chee softening, and copy audio out loud every day. Want the full audio pack to shadow? It’s inside the Survival Kit below.

Frequently asked questions

What's the hardest sound in Brazilian Portuguese?

For most English speakers it's the nasal vowels (ão, ãe, õe). There's no English equivalent, so your ear has to build it from scratch. The [nasal sounds guide](/speak-like-yourself/blog/portuguese-nasal-sounds/) drills them one at a time.

Why does the Brazilian R sound like an H?

At the start of a word or when doubled, the R is a guttural sound close to a strong English H, Rio = "HEE-oo." A single R between vowels is a light tap instead. See [the Brazilian R sound](/speak-like-yourself/blog/brazilian-portuguese-r-sound/).

Do I need to roll my Rs in Brazilian Portuguese?

No. That's more European Spanish/Portuguese. Brazilian Portuguese uses the H-like R and the soft tap, not a rolled R, so you can relax.
