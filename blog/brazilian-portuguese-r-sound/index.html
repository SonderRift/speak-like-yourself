The Brazilian R: When It Sounds Like an H

The Brazilian Portuguese R sound explained with audio: when the R sounds like an English H, when it's a soft tap, and how to know which is which.

Rafael · Brazilian Portuguese teacherAugust 11, 2026 · 2 min read

The Brazilian Portuguese R sound shocks almost every beginner, because the letter R often sounds like an English H. Rio is “HEE-oo,” not “REE-oh.” But sometimes R is a soft tap instead. The rule for which is which is simple once someone shows you, and getting it right is one of the fastest ways to sound Brazilian. Let me break it down with audio.

The two jobs of the letter R

Brazilian Portuguese has, in effect, two R sounds:

  1. The strong, H-like R (a breathy sound from the throat), and
  2. The soft tap (a quick flick of the tongue, like the middle of the American “water”).

Which one you use depends only on where the R sits in the word.

When R sounds like H

Use the strong, H-like R when the R is:

  • at the start of a word: rato (HAH-too), Rio (HEE-oo), rua (HOO-ah)
  • doubled (rr): carro (KAH-hoo), cachorro (dog)

Think of a soft English “h” with a little rasp. Do not roll it, that is more Spanish or European Portuguese.

When R is a soft tap

A single R between vowels is a light tap: caro (expensive) is “KAH-roo,” para is “PAH-rah,” hora is “OH-rah.” This is the easy one for English speakers, since it is close to the quick “d/t” sound in “ladder” or “water.”

What the final R actually does

Here the truth splits in two, and it is worth getting right, because the common “just drop it” advice is only half correct.

For verbs in their infinitive form, the final R really does disappear in casual speech across most of Brazil. Falar (to speak) becomes “fah-LAH,” comer becomes “koh-MEH,” beber becomes “beh-BEH.” Brazilians do this constantly in everyday talk, and copying it makes you sound relaxed and natural. In careful or formal speech the R often comes back, so it is a register choice, not a fixed rule.

For other words that end in R, like the nouns amor (love), mar (sea) or cor (color), the R usually stays put. Its exact sound shifts a little from one region to another, but it does not vanish the way it does on infinitive verbs.

The safe habit: drop the R on infinitive verbs when you want to sound casual, but keep it on nouns and let it take whichever regional color you are learning. This sits right next to the de and ti softening in the pronunciation guide.

The takeaway

The Brazilian R has two jobs: an H-like sound at the start or when doubled, and a soft tap between vowels. On top of that, the R ending infinitive verbs is often dropped in casual speech, while the R ending nouns stays put and simply shifts sound by region. Frequently asked questions

Why does the R in Rio sound like an H?

At the start of a word (and when doubled), the Brazilian R is a breathy, H-like sound from the throat, so Rio is "HEE-oo" and carro is "KAH-hoo." A single R between vowels is a soft tap instead.

Do you roll your Rs in Brazilian Portuguese?

No. The strong R is H-like, not a rolled/trilled R (that's more Spanish and European Portuguese). The other R is a light tap. Hear both above.

Is it okay to drop the final R?

Yes, it's very natural in casual Brazilian speech: falar becomes "fah-LAH." You'll be understood either way, but dropping it sounds more local. See the [pronunciation guide](/speak-like-yourself/blog/brazilian-portuguese-pronunciation/).
