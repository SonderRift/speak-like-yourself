Por Que, Porque, Por Quê and Porquê: The Four Porques Made Simple

A clear breakdown of the four porques, when each one appears, and examples that make the spelling choice feel less random.

RafaelBrazilian Portuguese teacher · September 10, 2026 · 9 min read
On this page

Por que porque difference trips up learners the moment they try to write a simple question, and honestly it trips up plenty of Brazilians too. Portuguese has four ways to write what sounds almost the same out loud: por que, por quê, porque, and porquê. Here’s the good news: there are only four, each has one clear job, and once you see the pattern you’ll never have to guess again. This gives you a quick rule for each one, real examples with English glosses, and a two-second test you can run in your head.

Why there are four in the first place

Say them out loud and they blur together. On paper they don’t. Two are written apart (por que, por quê), and two are written as one word (porque, porquê). Inside each pair, one carries an accent and one doesn’t. That’s the whole system. The accent and the space are doing all the work, so those two little details are exactly what you learn.

Here’s the map before we go one by one.

Form Written Job Rough English
por que two words, no accent asking a question why / for what reason
por quê two words, accent asking, at the end why?
porque one word, no accent giving a reason because
porquê one word, accent a noun the reason

Por que: the question opener

Use por que (two words, no accent) when you’re asking a question and something follows it. This is the one you’ll use most as a beginner, because it’s how you ask “why” at the start of a sentence.

  • Por que você está feliz? (Why are you sad?)
  • Por que o Brasil é tão grande? (Why is Brazil so big?)
  • Não sei por que ela saiu. (I don’t know why she left.)

Notice the last one isn’t a direct question, but it’s still asking, just tucked inside the sentence. Same por que, two words, no accent. If you’re building questions from scratch, this pairs naturally with the other question words covered in how to ask questions in Brazilian Portuguese.

Por quê: the question that ends there

Use por quê (two words, with accent) when “why” lands at the end, right before the question mark or a period. When the word is stressed at the end of a phrase, Portuguese adds the accent.

  • Você não vai à festa? Por quê? (Are you not going to the party? Why?)
  • Ela desistiu, e eu nem sei por quê. (She gave up, and I don’t even know why.)

Small word, big signal. If nothing comes after it, it gets the hat.

Quick tip

The accent lands on the last word of a phrase because that's where your voice naturally rises and stops. Say "por quê?" out loud and you'll hear the stress fall on that final sound. The accent just writes down what your mouth is already doing.

Porque: your everyday “because”

Use porque (one word, no accent) to give a reason. This is the answer to all those questions above, and you’ll say it constantly.

  • Estou feliz porque hoje é sexta. (I’m happy because today is Friday.)
  • Ela saiu porque estava cansada. (She left because she was tired.)
  • Não fui porque estava chovendo. (I didn’t go because it was raining.)

Question and answer often sit side by side, which makes the pair easy to feel:

  • Por que você estuda português? Porque eu amo o Brasil. (Why do you study Portuguese? Because I love Brazil.)

One asks, one answers. Two words for the question, one word for the reason.

Porquê: the noun you’ll use least

Use porquê (one word, with accent) as a noun meaning “the reason” or “the why.” You can spot it because an article (o, um) usually sits right in front of it, and you could swap in “the reason” in English.

  • Eu quero entender o porquê disso. (I want to understand the reason for this.)
  • Ela não explicou o porquê. (She didn’t explain the reason.)

You’ll meet this one far less often than the other three, so don’t lose sleep over it. Just recognize it when you read it.

The two-second test

When you’re stuck, run this in your head. It gets you the right form almost every time.

Ask yourself If yes Use
Am I asking why, with words after it? yes por que
Am I asking why, and it ends the phrase? yes por quê
Am I giving a reason? yes porque
Can I say “the reason” here (with o/um)? yes porquê

Two words to ask, one word to answer. Nail that, and you’ve got eighty percent of it.

That single line covers the two forms you’ll actually reach for every day. The accents on por quê and porquê are the polish you add once the split between asking and answering feels automatic. Beginners who get the two-word/one-word split first, and worry about the hats later, learn this faster and stress less.

If spelling rules like this feel like a lot right now, that’s normal, and it’s a sign you’re paying attention to the written language, which most learners skip. It fits into the bigger picture laid out in the beginner’s roadmap, where reading and writing come after your ears are warmed up. And once you’re chatting for real, you’ll hear porque glued to natural speech alongside the filler words that make you sound like a person instead of a textbook.

The takeaway

Four porques, four jobs, one pattern: written apart means you’re asking, written together means you’re answering, and the accent shows up when the word ends the phrase or turns into a noun. Practice the two-second test on a few real sentences and it stops being a rule you recall and becomes something you just feel. Get the two-word versus one-word split locked in first, and the accents will follow. If you want to keep building from here, take the next step below.
