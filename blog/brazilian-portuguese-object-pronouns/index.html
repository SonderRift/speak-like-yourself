Te Amo or Amo-te? Object Pronouns Brazilians Actually Use

Where object pronouns really land in spoken Brazilian Portuguese, why 'te amo' beats 'amo-te', and how to place me, te, and o without sounding like a textbook.

RafaelBrazilian Portuguese teacher · September 15, 2026 · 10 min read

Brazilian Portuguese object pronouns are where a lot of grammar books lose you and Brazilians quietly go the easy way. The textbook wants you to worry about o, a, lhe, and pronouns glued to the ends of verbs. Real speech runs on a much shorter list: mostly me and te, dropped right in front of the verb. Here’s what this gives you: the pronouns Brazilians actually say, where they sit in the sentence, the ones you can safely ignore, and a handful of phrases like te amo and me liga you can start using today.

What an object pronoun even is

A subject does the action. An object receives it. In “she called me”, me is the object. Portuguese has a full set of these, but Brazilian speech leans hard on just a few, and it puts them in a spot that feels backwards to English speakers: usually before the verb.

Me liga means “call me”. Word for word it’s “me call”. Te amo is “I love you”, literally “you I-love”. Once you accept that the little pronoun comes first, half the battle is over.

This front-of-the-verb habit is also the clearest line between the two big Portuguese accents. Say te amo and you sound Brazilian. Say amo-te, with the pronoun stuck onto the back of the verb, and you sound European or bookish. That trick of hooking the pronoun onto the end (amo-te, ligue-me, chamo-o) is the default in Portugal, but in Brazil it’s reserved for formal writing. When in doubt here, put the little word first and you’ll be right almost every time.

The pronouns Brazilians actually use

Here’s the working set. Notice how short it is.

Pronoun Means Example English
me me Me ajuda? Can you help me?
te you Te amo. I love you.
nos us Ele nos viu. He saw us.
o / a him / her / it Eu o conheço. I know him.
se himself / herself / each other Eles se falam. They talk to each other.

In everyday Brazil, me and te do most of the heavy lifting. Nos shows up but often gets swapped for a gente elsewhere in the sentence. And o / a as an object pronoun? Brazilians mostly avoid it in speech, which we’ll get to.

Teacher's note

If you only drill two pronouns this month, make them me and te. They cover a huge slice of daily conversation, and putting them right before the verb is the single habit that makes you sound natural.

Where the pronoun goes

The safe, natural spot in Brazilian Portuguese is right before the verb.

Portuguese English
Me liga depois. Call me later.
Te vejo amanhã. See you tomorrow.
Me passa o sal? Pass me the salt?
Ela me chamou. She called me.
Eu te avisei. I warned you.

Books will tell you a sentence “shouldn’t” start with me or te. Ignore that for speech. Starting with Me liga is exactly what Brazilians say. The grammar-prize version, Ligue-me, sounds like a formal letter, not a friend texting you back, and (as we saw up top) it’s the Portugal-style amo-te pattern rather than everyday Brazilian.

The tricky one: him, her, it

Here’s where learners overthink things. The “correct” object pronouns for him, her, and it are o and a, and they can even change shape after certain verb endings (conhecê-lo, “to know him”). It’s fussy, and Brazilians in casual speech mostly route around it.

The everyday fix is simple: use the subject pronoun ele / ela, or just drop the object entirely because it’s obvious.

Textbook version What Brazilians say English
Eu o vi ontem. Eu vi ele ontem. I saw him yesterday.
Você a conhece? Você conhece ela? Do you know her?
Eu não o comprei. Eu não comprei. I didn’t buy it.

Yes, vi ele is technically frowned on by strict grammarians. And yes, it’s what you’ll hear from Rio to São Paulo every single day. Understand o and a when you read them, but you don’t need to force them out of your own mouth.

When the action loops back: reflexives

Sometimes the pronoun points back at the person doing the action. That’s a reflexive, and Brazilian speech uses the same front-of-the-verb habit. Se covers “himself”, “herself”, “themselves”, and “each other”, while me and te double as “myself” and “yourself”.

Portuguese English
Me chamo Rafael. My name is (I call myself) Rafael.
Ela se arrumou. She got (herself) ready.
A gente se fala. We’ll talk (to each other).
Se cuida! Take care (of yourself)!

Notice Me chamo and Se cuida start with the pronoun too, exactly like Me liga. Same instinct, so once the object habit clicks, reflexives come almost free.

Pronouns after prepositions

When the pronoun follows a little word like com (with), pra (for/to), or de (of), it changes form. This trips people up because me and te disappear here.

After a preposition English
pra mim for me
com você with you
pra você for you
com a gente with us
de mim of / about me

So it’s Me liga (before the verb), but Liga pra mim (after the preposition). Same idea, two shapes. When you’re setting up plans and asking someone to reach you, this is the pattern you’ll reuse constantly, so it pairs naturally with learning how Brazilians make plans and invitations.

Ready-made phrases to steal

Don’t build these from grammar. Learn them whole, then swap one word.

  • Te amo, I love you
  • Me liga, Call me
  • Me manda, Send me (a message, a photo)
  • Me diz uma coisa, Tell me something
  • Me espera!, Wait for me!
  • Te ligo depois, I’ll call you later
  • Te vejo mais tarde, I’ll see you later
  • Deixa eu te falar, Let me tell you (a very Brazilian opener)

Te amo leads straight into the softer vocabulary Brazilians layer on top, so it sits close to the terms of endearment you’ll want next.

The whole secret is unglamorous: pick me and te, park them in front of the verb, and stop worrying about o and a. That one move takes you from “grammatically correct and stiff” to “sounds like you live here”.

The takeaway

Brazilian object pronouns look scary on a chart and turn out tiny in real life. Lean on me and te, put them before the verb, swap in ele / ela when o and a feel awkward, and switch to mim and você after prepositions.
